What Stronghold is

A kingdom-management game that takes one move a day and seven months to finish.

You rule one province. There are ninety-six of them, grouped into twelve realms of eight, and yours is not the important one. The crown of your own realm sits on somebody else's head — an AI lord with his own ambitions, his own opinion of you, and no particular interest in your advancement.

The game is what you do about that.

One move a day

Stronghold takes exactly one turn per weekday. You open it, read what happened overnight, give the day's orders, and submit. That is the whole session — five minutes, sometimes fifteen. The next turn is tomorrow.

Weekends the court does not sit. A day you miss is gone; there is no catching up, no stockpiling, no playing three turns because you had a free evening. A campaign runs about a hundred and fifty committed days, which is six or seven months of real calendar.

This is the whole design, not a limitation of it. A decision you cannot immediately undo is a different kind of decision. Sending your army out means being without it tomorrow, and knowing it, and going to work anyway.

You are not told the numbers that would make it easy

Most strategy games hand you a combat calculator. Stronghold does not show offense, defense, prestige or efficiency anywhere, at any zoom. There is no figures toggle.

What you get instead is people. Your marshal tells you a province is properly garrisoned, and this is a few days old. Your spymaster is confident, or he is guessing. Counts a clerk would read from a ledger stay numbers — coin, grain, acres, muster rolls, days. Judgements a person would form come back as words.

Three wagons of them outside Kelm, half without boots.

So you decide whether a war is winnable the way the job actually works: from what the people around you say, knowing some of them are wrong.

The world is dark until somebody tells you

You begin knowing your own realm and nothing else. The other eleven appear as you hear of them — a letter, a line in the chronicle, a dossier your agents bring back, an army on your border. Until then that part of the map is blank water.

It gates action, not just cartography. You cannot march on, spy on, curse or treat with a realm nobody has ever mentioned to you. Word spreads by proximity: three quarters of what you hear concerns your own neighbourhood. Knowing the whole world takes about eleven weeks.

How it ends

Your kingdom holds every acre in the world, and then holds it for ten more days.

That sounds like four hundred sieges, and once it was. It is not any more, because realms surrender as realms. Take enough of a kingdom's ground and break the seat of its crown and the rest of its houses read the room; or send an embassy, when you are plainly the greater power, and ask. The war map is eleven decisions rather than seven hundred fights.

Getting there means climbing from Lord to Councilor to Regent to Crown, which is done with standing at court — earned by winning battles, answering the Crown's dues, and being the sort of neighbour other houses remember.

The world writes to you

Every campaign has a cast: a marshal, a chancellor, a spymaster, an arcanist, a steward, an envoy, all named at founding and there for the whole seven months. Soldiers write home. Merchants complain about tolls. Villages petition about the levy.

Every letter is anchored to something that actually happened in the simulation — the grain rumour needs a genuinely hungry province — and no letter prints a statistic. There are 4,761 written fragments behind a hundred situations, assembled by event, sender, your relationship with them, what they remember, and their voice, so the same defeat reads differently from a proud rival than from a nervous stranger.

What it costs

Nothing. There are a hundred seats because that is what one person can afford to host, and a seat opens the moment another ruler ends their campaign. Nothing in the game can be bought, and no payment improves your position in any way.
