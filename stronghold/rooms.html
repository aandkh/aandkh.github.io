The fifteen rooms

Medieval administration through a modern command terminal — dark, dense, textual, and deliberately not a dashboard.

The daily business

Command

The day's ledger and the submit button. Acres, souls, coin, grain, standing, and how many of the world's ninety-six houses answer to your realm. Your council speaks here — four officers, one line each, on whatever they think matters this morning.

Province

Your own ground. Taxes, the draft and wages are standing policy and hold until you change them. Eighteen kinds of building compete for open land: farms feed, housing raises the ceiling, markets pay, armouries make training cheaper, watch posts make you harder to read. Surveyors clear new ground, at a price that climbs steeply the larger you get.

Military

The yard and the column. Infantry, guards, retainers and agents train from your recruits; two generals mean at most two columns on the road. Seven kinds of campaign, from a raid that comes home in a day with somebody's coin, to a conquest that takes ground, to a countermarch that catches an army on the road rather than the walls behind it.

Intel

Sixteen operations, from surveying a province to robbing its treasury. What they buy you is confidence: a fresh dossier turns a forty-five per cent guess about somebody's walls into a six per cent one, and that difference is usually what decides whether a column marches at all.

Arcana

Sixteen workings, treated as one more department with a budget. Some improve your own harvest, coin or defences; the hostile ones wither a rival's growth or shatter their supply lines. Mana returns slowly enough that this is a decision every second day, not every day.

Research

Ten fields, funded by how you split your scholars' attention. Fields finish, at which point money poured into them is visibly wasted and the split has to move. Every so often the scholars bring a breakthrough — twenty-six exist, a campaign takes perhaps fifteen, and the ones you turn down are not offered again.

Land, food and money

Harvest opens about a month in

Hamurabi (1968) entire, inside a medieval province. One stock of grain, three ways to spend it: the people eat it, the fields take it as seed, and the market turns it into land or coin. Two bushels sow an acre, nobody can work more than ten acres, an acre returns one to five bushels, and an acre of land costs seventeen to twenty-seven grain. The prices move under you. Occasionally the rats get in.

Grain is not a resource you accumulate here. It is the economy.

The quiet trade inside the Harvest

A second crop, deliberately never named anywhere you can read it. The court calls it the crop; the arcanist calls it what it is and is ignored. It grows where grain grows, is worth many times grain abroad, and is worth almost nothing at home — your own realm-mates are neither rich enough nor willing, which is the point: the money has to come from strangers.

A province that takes a cargo earns a tenth less the following day. Do that to a realm often enough and you are not trading with them.

Other people

Banners opens in about a week

A single province can never out-muscle a realm, so you ask your neighbours. Lords who think well of you send men; their men ride for their wages and for a favour owed, which they will come and collect. The other half of the room is the post: those same houses ask you for grain, coin and troops, and answering is the cheapest standing in the game, because the whole realm can see you do it.

Kingdom the Court opens around day 20

Your realm and your place in it. Aid a house that is short, put coin into the realm's projects, and — once the Court opens — give your six officers actual posts. An officer settles into a job over about six months and is worth three times as much at the end of it, so moving somebody has a real cost.

Accord opens once you have heard of somebody

Terms with foreign realms, one embassy a day. Buy a truce and they may still refuse. Hand over your intelligence as a one-sided gift and hope it is remembered. Or, when you are plainly the greater power, demand a realm's submission — which is how a world is actually taken. Foreign courts write to you here too: a war they are losing, protection money from a neighbour who can read a map, terms offered before you come and take them.

Dues opens about two months in

The Crown's summons. Obligations arrive through the month — coin, grain, men under arms, good order kept — and nothing settles until the reckoning. Meeting every duty of one category is worth far more than the same number scattered across four, and sweeping all four pays again. This is the faucet that turns governing well into standing at court.

Reading the world

Diplomacy

Where everyone stands with everyone, and the day's dispatches: whose war has stopped moving, which border got worse overnight, who has been asked to sign what.

World

A globe generated from your campaign's seed — four crowded landmasses, deliberately not Earth's arrangement. Realms are drawn as countries with their houses ringed tightly around their seat. Geography is fixed: land changes hands, coastlines do not, and a province taken is simply repainted in its new colours. Realms you have not heard of are not on it.

Chronicle

The house book, written up each evening after everyone has gone home. Battles, harvests, appointments, rats in the granary, a realm coming in whole. On a quiet day it records the count and nothing else, which is also true.

Mail

Your correspondence. The recurring cast of your court, foreign rulers who remember exactly how you have treated them, soldiers writing home, merchants on the tolls, villages on the levy. Six or seven letters most mornings, none of them printing a number at you.
